Conditions That Prompt Trade

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 3.2.1 - Conditions That Prompt Trade. Students need to explain the push and pull factors that lead a firm to sell abroad, the chance to offshore or outsource, and how selling in more markets can extend a product's life cycle and raise capacity utilisation.

Why Firms Look Abroad

Most firms start by selling in their home country. The reasons a firm starts to sell abroad fall into two groups.

Push factor: a problem in the home market that drives a firm to look for sales abroad.

Pull factor: a benefit of selling abroad that attracts a firm to expand there.

Push Factors

Saturated Markets

Saturated market: a market where most people who want the product already have it, so sales grow slowly and come mainly from replacements.

Almost every adult in the UK owns a smartphone, so a phone maker can only grow at home by taking customers from rivals. In a country where ownership is still rising, every new buyer is extra demand.

Saturated home market → few new customers → sales and revenue stop growing → the firm sells in countries where demand is still rising → higher sales and profit.

Competition

When rivals at home cut prices or new firms enter, margins shrink. A market abroad with fewer competitors can be more profitable.

More rivals at home → price cuts to keep customers → lower margins and profit → the firm sells where it faces less competition and can charge more.

Pull Factors

Economies of Scale

Selling in more countries lets a firm produce more, and a larger scale of output brings lower average costs (unit 2.1.1).

New export markets → higher output → bulk buying, and design and advertising costs spread over more units → lower average cost → lower prices or higher profit per unit.

Risk Spreading

A firm that sells in one country depends on that one economy. Selling in several means a fall in demand in one market can be made up by sales elsewhere.

A recession hits the UK → UK sales fall, but sales in other countries hold up → revenue and cash flow stay steadier → less risk of losses and job cuts.

This works best when the markets don't move together. Economies are more interdependent than they used to be (unit 3.1.3), so a global recession can hit every market at once.

Offshoring and Outsourcing

Firms also trade by buying from abroad: they move parts of their production to countries where it is cheaper.

Offshoring: moving part of a firm's production or services to another country.

Outsourcing: paying another firm to carry out work the business used to do itself.

The two can go together. A UK clothing brand that pays a factory in Vietnam to make its shirts is both offshoring and outsourcing. A UK bank that moves its call centre to its own office in India is offshoring but not outsourcing.

When a firm offshores or outsources:

  • Costs: wages and rents are lower abroad → lower unit costs → the firm can cut prices or earn a higher margin.
  • Focus: a specialist supplier does the routine work → the firm puts its staff and money into what it does best, such as design → a stronger product.
  • UK workers: jobs move abroad → staff are made redundant → lower incomes in the areas the work left.

Whether it pays depends on:

  • Control over quality: it is harder to check work done by another firm thousands of miles away. Faults or poor working conditions at a supplier can damage the brand.
  • The length of the supply chain: goods take weeks to ship, so the firm reacts more slowly to changes in demand, and a blocked port can halt supplies.
  • Whether the cost gap lasts: as wages rise in emerging economies (unit 3.1.1), the saving shrinks, and some firms bring work back home.

Offshoring is about where; outsourcing is about who. Offshoring moves work to another country. Outsourcing hands it to another firm, which may be in the same town. Read the extract carefully to see which one the firm has done, or whether it has done both, and use the right term.

Extending the Product Life Cycle

A product can be in maturity or decline in one country and only just launching in another (unit 2.1.3). Selling in a new market is an extension strategy: it starts the life cycle again.

UK sales start to fall → the firm launches the product in a country where it is new → sales grow again → more revenue from a product whose development is already paid for → cash to fund new products.

This works when the product is new to customers abroad. A product in decline because better technology has replaced it is likely to decline everywhere.

Raising Capacity Utilisation

A firm with spare capacity has premises and machinery standing idle (unit 2.3.2). Orders from abroad can fill it.

Export orders → higher output from the same factory → fixed costs spread over more units → lower average cost and higher profit.

Exports can also smooth out a seasonal business. A UK maker of garden furniture is quiet in the British winter, which is summer in Australia.

How much selling abroad helps depends on:

  • The cost of entering the market: marketing, shipping and adapting the product for a new country can cost more than the extra sales bring in.
  • Exchange rates: a stronger pound makes UK exports dearer abroad and cuts the value of foreign sales in pounds (unit 2.4.4).
  • How much spare capacity there is: a firm already near full capacity would need a new factory to meet foreign orders, which removes the cost advantage.
