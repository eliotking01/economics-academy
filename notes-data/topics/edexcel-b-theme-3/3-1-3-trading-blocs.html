Trading Blocs

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 3.1.3 - Trading Blocs. Students need to explain trade creation and trade diversion, describe the growth of the EU single market, ASEAN and NAFTA, analyse the impact of trading blocs on firms and explain how they make economies more interdependent.

What a Trading Bloc Is

Trading bloc: a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove barriers to trade between themselves (unit 2.4.3).

Blocs differ in how closely their members work together. Each type in the table includes everything in the one above it.

Type What members agree Example
Free trade area No tariffs or quotas on trade between members; each sets its own tariffs on everyone else USMCA (formerly NAFTA)
Customs union Also a common external tariff: the same tariff on imports from outside, whichever member they enter Mercosur (South America)
Single market Also free movement of services, capital and workers, and shared rules on products The EU
Monetary union Also a single currency and one central bank The Eurozone (unit 3.1.5)

Trade Creation and Trade Diversion

Trade creation: when joining a bloc moves trade from a higher-cost producer at home to a lower-cost producer in another member country.

Trade diversion: when joining a bloc moves trade from a lower-cost producer outside the bloc to a higher-cost producer inside it.

Trade creation is a gain: goods are made where they cost least, and consumers pay less. Trade diversion is a loss of efficiency, because the tariff, not the cost of making the good, decides where it is bought.

Worked Example: Buying Televisions

A country puts a 20% tariff on every imported television. A television costs £100 to make in China, £110 in Germany and £125 at home. The country then joins a bloc with Germany but not China.

Producer Price before joining Price after joining
China £100 + 20% = £120 £120 (tariff still applies)
Germany £110 + 20% = £132 £110 (no tariff)
Home producer £125 £125

Before joining, buyers choose China at £120. Afterwards they switch to Germany at £110. That is trade diversion: the televisions now come from a producer whose costs are £10 higher. Consumers still pay £10 less, but the government loses £20 of tariff revenue on each one. Had buyers switched from the home producer instead, it would have been trade creation.

Expansion of Trading Blocs

The EU and the Single Market

Single market: a trading bloc where goods, services, capital and people move freely between members, under one set of product rules.

The EU's single market began in 1993 with 12 members. It grew to 28, with ten countries joining in 2004 alone, and has 27 since the UK left.

The UK left the single market and customs union at the end of 2020. Its trade deal with the EU keeps goods free of tariffs and quotas if they meet the deal's rules of origin, but exporters now fill in customs declarations and face border checks. In December 2024 the UK joined the CPTPP, a trade agreement with Pacific countries including Japan and Australia.

ASEAN

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) was set up in 1967 by five countries, including Indonesia and Singapore. It had ten members by 1999, and Timor-Leste became the eleventh in 2025. Since 1992, members have cut most tariffs on trade with each other, so a firm with a factory in one member, such as Vietnam, can sell across the region.

NAFTA

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) removed most tariffs between the US, Canada and Mexico from 1994. It was replaced in July 2020 by the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), which keeps most trade tariff-free but requires more of a car's parts to be made in North America before it qualifies.

Impact on Firms of Trading Blocs

For firms inside a bloc:

  • A bigger market: tariffs on sales to other members go → firms sell to millions more customers at competitive prices → higher output brings economies of scale → lower unit costs and higher profit.
  • Cheaper inputs: parts and materials from other members arrive tariff-free → lower costs → lower prices or higher margins.
  • More workers to hire: in a single market, workers can move between members → firms can fill vacancies from across the bloc → fewer skills shortages and less pressure on wages.
  • More competition: rivals from other members sell freely in the firm's home market → prices are pushed down → inefficient firms lose sales and profit, and some close.

For firms outside a bloc:

  • Facing the external tariff: their goods pay a tariff that members' goods don't → they are less competitive in the bloc → they lose sales, or set up a factory inside the bloc to get round the tariff.

The impact on a firm depends on:

  • The type of bloc: in a free trade area, firms must prove where their goods were made, which adds paperwork. A single market removes most border checks and sets one set of product rules.
  • How much it trades with members: a firm selling only at home gains little from tariff-free access and still faces new competition.

Growing Interdependence

Interdependence: when countries rely on each other for trade, investment and supplies, so that what happens in one economy affects the others.

Blocs deepen interdependence, because members trade more with each other and build supply chains that cross their borders. The EU buys about two-fifths of the UK's exports (Department for Business and Trade), so:

  • A recession spreads: EU incomes fall → EU households and firms buy fewer UK exports → UK output falls → UK firms cut jobs.
  • A supply shock spreads: a factory in one member stops producing parts → firms in other members that rely on them cannot finish their goods → output falls across the bloc.

Interdependence also cuts the other way: when the bloc grows, every member's exporters gain. And countries that depend on each other's trade have a reason to settle disputes by negotiation.

Creation or diversion depends on where the goods came from before. Trade creation means buying from a member instead of a dearer home producer; trade diversion means buying from a member instead of a cheaper outside producer. In both cases consumers usually pay less, so don't call diversion bad for consumers. Its cost is lost efficiency and lost tariff revenue.
