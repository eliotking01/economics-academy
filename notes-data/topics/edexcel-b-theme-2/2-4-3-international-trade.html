International Trade

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 2.4.3 - International Trade. Students need to explain how specialisation leads to trade, what trading blocs are, how trade affects economic growth, the difference between visible and invisible trade, and how cheap imports affect standards of living.

Specialisation and International Trade

Countries specialise for the same reason workers do: each gets better at what it focuses on, and trades for the rest (unit 1.2.4). The question is which goods a country should specialise in.

Absolute advantage: when one country can make more of a good than another country with the same quantity of resources.

Comparative advantage: when one country's opportunity cost of making a good is lower than another country's, so it gives up less of other goods to make it.

The theory of comparative advantage says each country should specialise in the goods it makes at the lowest opportunity cost, then trade. World output rises, and every country can end up with more than it could make alone.

Worked Example: Germany and Vietnam

Using the figures in Figure 1, the opportunity cost of one good is what the country gives up of the other.

Step Germany Vietnam
Opportunity cost of 1 chip 150 ÷ 20 = 7.5 T-shirts 200 ÷ 10 = 20 T-shirts
Opportunity cost of 1 T-shirt 20 ÷ 150 = 0.13 chips 10 ÷ 200 = 0.05 chips
Output with resources split equally 10mn chips and 75mn T-shirts 5mn chips and 100mn T-shirts
Output when fully specialised 20mn chips 200mn T-shirts

Germany has the lower opportunity cost for chips and Vietnam for T-shirts. Split equally, the two make 15mn chips and 175mn T-shirts between them. Fully specialised, they make 20mn chips and 200mn T-shirts: 5mn more chips and 25mn more T-shirts from the same resources. Both gain from trading as long as a chip swaps for between 7.5 and 20 T-shirts.

Real-world gains are smaller than the model suggests:

  • Transport costs and trade barriers eat into the extra output.
  • Resources do not switch easily. Workers and machines in a declining industry cannot move into the growing one overnight.
  • Over-specialisation is risky. A country that relies on one or two exports is hit hard if demand or prices for them fall.

Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not being best. A country that is better at making everything still gains from trade, by specialising in the goods where its lead is largest and importing the rest. In an answer, work out the opportunity cost for each country before saying who should specialise in what.

Trading Blocs

Trading bloc: a group of countries that agree to reduce or remove barriers to trade between themselves, such as the EU.

Firms inside a bloc can sell to the other members without tariffs, which gives them a larger home market. Firms outside it can face tariffs and extra paperwork, which is why many foreign firms set up inside a bloc to sell to it. The types of bloc and their costs and benefits are covered in unit 3.1.3.

Trade and Growth

Trade can raise a country's economic growth in four ways:

  • Exports: foreign demand for UK goods rises → UK firms sell more and expand output → they hire more workers, whose spending raises demand further → higher real GDP (unit 4.4.1).
  • Bigger markets: firms sell to the world, not just one country → they produce on a larger scale → economies of scale lower their average costs → more competitive prices and higher profits to invest.
  • Competition: imports compete with home producers → firms must cut costs and improve products to survive → higher productivity, which raises the economy's capacity to grow.
  • Access to inputs and technology: firms can import cheaper materials and better machinery → lower costs and higher output per worker.

It works the other way too: as incomes grow, people buy more, including more imports, so growth itself raises trade.

How much trade adds to growth depends on:

  • Imports as well as exports: spending on imports leaves the country, so if imports replace home production, the gain from exports is partly offset.
  • What the country exports: a country that relies on raw materials, whose prices swing widely, gets less steady growth than one exporting a mix of goods and services.
  • Trade barriers: tariffs abroad, or retaliation in a trade dispute, limit how much a country can sell.

Imports and Exports: Visibles and Invisibles

Exports: goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in another, so money flows into the exporting country.

Imports: goods and services bought from another country, so money flows out.

Visible trade: trade in goods, things you can see and touch, such as cars, medicines or oil.

Invisible trade: trade in services, such as banking, insurance, tourism and education.

What matters is which way the money flows. An American tourist spending money in London is an invisible export for the UK, because money comes in. A UK family on holiday in Spain is an invisible import. The UK imports more goods than it exports but exports more services than it imports (ONS).

Impact of Cheap Imports on Standards of Living

Standard of living: the quantity and quality of goods and services people can afford, which depends on their real income.

When cheap imports come into the UK:

  • Consumers gain: imported clothes, food and electronics cost less → each pound buys more → real incomes rise → a higher standard of living, with more choice too.
  • Firms using imported inputs gain: cheaper materials and components → lower costs → lower prices for their own customers, or higher profit.
  • Competing UK producers lose: cheap imports win customers from them → sales and profits fall → factories close and jobs go → lower incomes for workers in those industries and the towns that depend on them.

Whether living standards rise overall depends on:

  • Whether workers find new jobs: if they can retrain or move into growing industries, the loss is short term. If their skills don't fit and jobs are elsewhere, they can stay unemployed for years.
  • Why the imports are cheap: low prices from lower costs or better methods are a genuine gain. Prices kept low by poor safety standards or by selling below cost to win a market can hurt consumers and UK firms later.
  • How much people spend on imports: households that spend a large share of their income on imported goods gain most from lower prices.
