Role of an Entrepreneur

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.2.1 - Role of an Entrepreneur in the Economy. Students need to explain creative destruction, the decisions an entrepreneur makes to operate, expand and develop a business, and how a business adds value by selling its output for more than its inputs cost.

What an Entrepreneur Does

Entrepreneur: a person who spots a business opportunity, brings together the resources needed to exploit it and accepts the risk that it might fail.

Land, labour and capital do nothing on their own. Someone has to decide what to make, find the premises, hire the staff, buy the equipment and put up the money. That organising and risk-taking is enterprise, the fourth factor of production (unit 1.2.3), and the entrepreneur is the person who supplies it. If the business works, their reward is profit. If it doesn't, they can lose what they put in.

The spec gives the entrepreneur three jobs: starting new enterprises that shake up markets, making the decisions that run and grow a business, and adding value.

Creative Destruction

Creative destruction: the process by which new products, methods and firms replace older ones, as resources move away from businesses that have fallen behind and into new ones.

The idea comes from the Austrian-born economist Joseph Schumpeter. In his 1942 book Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy, he argued that this constant churn is how capitalist economies grow. The entrepreneur drives it. By organising the factors of production into a new enterprise, they offer customers something better or cheaper than before, and spending shifts towards them.

The process has two halves:

  • Creative: new firms, new jobs and new products appear, and consumers get more choice and better value.
  • Destructive: firms that fail to adapt lose sales and close. Their workers, premises and capital are released for other uses, though workers whose skills were specific to the old industry may struggle to find new jobs.

For the economy as a whole, the churn is a gain over time. Labour and capital leave firms that use them poorly and end up in firms that use them well, so the country can produce more with the same resources.

Video rental is a clear UK case. Blockbuster rented out films on DVD from shops on British high streets. New online rivals took its customers: LOVEFiLM, a UK start-up that posted DVDs to your door and later streamed them, and then Netflix. When Blockbuster's UK business went into administration in January 2013 it still had 528 shops and about 4,200 staff. A rescue failed and it went into administration again that October. The jobs lost were real, but the spending had moved to firms offering a service customers preferred.

Creative destruction gives you both sides of an evaluation. Consumers and new firms gain, while workers and owners of the old firms lose, at least in the short run. Say who wins, who loses and over what time period, and you have a balanced answer built from one concept.

Decisions to Operate, Expand and Develop a Business

Setting up is only the start. An entrepreneur keeps making decisions, each one with an opportunity cost and a risk attached.

  • Operating the business day to day: what to sell, what price to charge, how many staff to employ, which suppliers to use and how to keep enough cash coming in to pay the bills.
  • Expanding it: producing more of what already works, for example opening a second shop, taking on a larger unit, selling online as well as in person, or buying a rival. Expansion usually needs finance, which adds debt or brings in new owners.
  • Developing it: changing what the business does, by launching new products, improving existing ones or moving into new markets. This is where innovation happens, and where the risk of getting it wrong is highest.

A small bakery shows the difference. Deciding tomorrow's bake is operating. Opening a second branch in the next town is expanding. Starting a wholesale line of gluten-free bread for local cafés is developing.

Adding Value

Added value: the difference between the price a business sells its output for and the cost of the inputs it buys in from other firms, such as raw materials and components.

A business only survives in the long run if customers will pay more for its output than its inputs cost. Entrepreneurs add value by turning inputs into something customers want more, through:

  • Design and quality that make the product better than the alternatives.
  • Branding, so customers trust the name and will pay more for it.
  • Convenience, such as delivery, longer opening hours or an easy online checkout.
  • A unique selling point (USP) that no rival offers.

Worked Example: Added Value on a Candle

A small business makes scented candles at home. For each candle it buys in wax, a wick, a glass jar and fragrance oil, then sells the finished candle online for £14.

Selling price £14.00
Bought-in inputs (wax, wick, jar, fragrance) £1.20 + £0.30 + £1.10 + £0.60 = £3.20
Added value per candle £14.00 − £3.20 = £10.80

The owner has turned £3.20 of materials into a product customers will pay £14 for. That £10.80 is not profit: it still has to pay for the owner's time, advertising, website fees and delivery. Profit is whatever is left after those costs too.
