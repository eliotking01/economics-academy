Business Objectives

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.1.2 - Business Objectives. Students need to explain profit maximisation, sales maximisation and satisficing and the reasons a business might choose each, then the other objectives the spec lists: survival, market share, cost efficiency, return on investment, employee welfare, customer satisfaction and social objectives.

What a Business Objective Is

Business objective:a target a business sets itself, which guides its decisions and gives it a way to judge how well it is doing.

Economic theory usually assumes firms want the most profit they can make. Real businesses are more varied. A new café may simply want to get through its first year, while a charity shop exists to raise money for a cause. The objective a business picks depends on who owns and runs it, how long it has been trading and the state of its market.

Profit Maximisation

Profit maximisation:producing the output and charging the price that make the gap between total revenue and total costs as large as possible.

Reasons a business might aim for it:

  • Profit is the reward to the owners. In a company it pays shareholders their dividends and pushes up the share price.
  • Profit kept in the business, called retained profit, is the cheapest way to pay for new equipment or expansion.
  • A profitable firm finds it easier to borrow and to attract new investors.

In practice few firms know their costs and demand well enough to find the exact profit-maximising point. The rule economists use to find it, using marginal cost and marginal revenue, comes later in the course (unit 4.1.4).

Sales Maximisation

Sales maximisation:selling as many units as possible without making a loss, so the business at least breaks even.

To sell more, a firm usually has to charge less, so it gives up some profit per unit. Reasons it might accept that trade-off:

  • A bigger share of the market makes the firm harder to ignore, for customers and for suppliers.
  • Higher output can bring economies of scale, lowering the cost of each unit over time.
  • Low prices can put off new firms thinking about entering the market.
  • Managers' pay and status often rise with the size of the business they run, not with its profit.

Charities and social enterprises that only need to cover their costs can work this way too, reaching as many people as their income allows.

Satisficing

Satisficing:aiming for a satisfactory level of profit that keeps the owners happy, instead of the most profit possible.

The main reason is the divorce of ownership and control. In a large public limited company the shareholders own the business, but salaried managers run it day to day. The managers' own goals - a higher salary, an easier working life, job security - may not match the owners' wish for maximum profit. As long as profit is good enough to stop shareholders complaining or selling up, managers can pursue those goals instead. This clash of interests is called the principal-agent problem, with the owners as principals and the managers as their agents.

Small businesses satisfice too. The owner of a small business may be happy with a comfortable income and decide an extra few thousand pounds of profit is not worth working every weekend. A firm with many stakeholders to keep on side may also settle for a profit that leaves room to pay staff more or keep prices down.

Other Objectives

The spec lists seven more objectives. A business can hold several at once, and one usually matters most at any given time.

Objective What it means Why a business might choose it
Survival Staying in business: covering costs and having the cash to pay bills when they fall due. Most important for start-ups, which often fail in their first few years, and for any firm in a recession or facing a new competitor.
Market share Increasing the firm's sales as a percentage of the total sales in its market. A bigger share can mean more influence over price, more power when bargaining with suppliers, and a brand that customers choose first.
Cost efficiency Producing the same output with fewer resources, so the cost of each unit falls. Lower costs allow lower prices or wider profit margins. This matters most where customers shop on price, as they do with budget airlines.
Return on investment The profit an investment earns as a percentage of what it cost. £1,500 profit from £10,000 invested is a 15% return. Owners and lenders compare returns when deciding where to put their money. A project earning less than a savings account is hard to justify.
Employee welfare Looking after staff through fair pay, training, safe conditions and job security. Well-treated staff tend to stay longer and work harder, which cuts the cost of recruiting and training replacements.
Customer satisfaction Meeting or beating what customers expect on quality, service and value. Satisfied customers come back and recommend the business. Online reviews make a reputation quick to build and quick to lose.
Social objectives Aims that benefit society or the environment, such as cutting emissions or employing people who struggle to find work. Some businesses exist for a social purpose. Others find a good reputation attracts customers and staff.

How Objectives Change

Objectives shift as a business grows and as conditions change. A start-up focuses on survival. Once it is established it may chase market share, and a mature firm in a stable market is more likely to target profit or return on investment. A recession can push even a large firm back to survival and cost efficiency.

Objectives can also pull against each other. Spending more on staff pay helps employee welfare but works against cost efficiency, at least in the short run. Cutting prices to win market share lowers profit per unit.

Tie the objective to the business in front of you. A question about a two-month-old start-up and one about a large public company rarely have the same answer. Use the firm's age, size, ownership and market from the extract to explain why one objective fits it better than another.
