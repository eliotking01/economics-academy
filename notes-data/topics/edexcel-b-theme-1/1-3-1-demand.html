Demand

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.3.1 - Demand. Students need to understand consumer objectives and decisions, the demand curve, the difference between a movement along the demand curve and a shift of it, and the factors that shift the curve.

Consumer Objectives and Decisions

Every consumer has a limited income and far more things they would like to buy than they can afford. Their objective is to get as much satisfaction as possible from the money they have. Economists call this satisfaction utility.

So each purchase is a decision. A student with £40 left at the end of the month might choose between a new pair of jeans, a night out and topping up their phone. Whatever they pick, the others are the opportunity cost. To decide, a consumer weighs the price against the satisfaction they expect, and price is only part of it: quality, convenience, the brand and their own values all count.

Each extra unit of the same thing usually adds less satisfaction than the one before. The third coffee of the day is worth less to you than the first, so you will only buy it if it is cheap enough.

The Demand Curve

Demand: the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a given period of time.

Both parts matter. Wanting a new car is not demand unless you can pay for it. Economists call demand that is backed by the ability to pay effective demand.

Law of demand: as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (all other things staying the same).

That is why the demand curve slopes down from left to right. When the price falls, buyers who could not afford it before can now, existing buyers tend to buy more, and the good looks cheaper next to its alternatives.

Movements Along and Shifts of the Demand Curve

A change in the good's own price moves consumers along the existing curve:

  • Extension of demand: the price falls and quantity demanded rises, a move down the curve (A to B in Figure 1).
  • Contraction of demand: the price rises and quantity demanded falls, a move up the curve (B to A).

A change in anything else that affects demand shifts the whole curve. An increase in demand moves it right: more is bought at every price. A decrease in demand moves it left.

A good's own price never shifts its own demand curve. If a question says the price of a product has risen, show a contraction along the curve, not a shift left. Keep the words apart as well: "extension" and "contraction" for movements, "increase" and "decrease in demand" for shifts.

Factors That Shift the Demand Curve

Two of the factors depend on how goods are related. Substitutes: goods that can be used in place of each other, such as train and coach travel. Complements: goods that are used together, such as a games console and its games.

Real income: income adjusted for inflation, so it measures what a consumer can actually buy. Most goods are normal goods, where demand rises as real income rises. A few are inferior goods, where demand falls as real income rises because people switch to something better.

Factor What increases demand Example
Price of a substitute The substitute's price rises Rail fares go up, so more people book coach tickets
Price of a complement The complement's price falls Consoles are discounted, so demand for games rises
Real incomes Real incomes rise (for a normal good) or fall (for an inferior good) Higher real incomes raise demand for holidays abroad but cut demand for supermarket value ranges
Tastes and fashions The product becomes more popular A style of trainer goes viral on social media
Advertising and branding A campaign works, or the brand becomes better known and trusted A trusted brand name persuades shoppers to choose it over cheaper alternatives
Size and age distribution of the population The population grows, or the age group that buys the product gets larger As the UK population ages, demand for care homes and mobility aids rises

Each change works in reverse too. A cheaper substitute or a product going out of fashion shifts demand to the left.
