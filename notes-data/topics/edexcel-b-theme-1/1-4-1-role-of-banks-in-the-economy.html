Role of Banks in the Economy

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.4.1 - Role of Banks in the Economy. Students need to understand how banks channel savings towards investment, how they provide credit to firms and households, and how interest rates and collateral affect the cost of borrowing.

Channelling Savings Towards Investment

Some households and firms have more money than they want to spend right now. Others want to spend more than they have. A bank brings the two groups together.

Financial intermediary: an organisation, such as a bank, that takes in money from savers and lends it to borrowers.

Savers deposit money with a bank and are paid interest on it. The bank lends that money on to firms that want to invest - to buy machinery, open a new site or develop a product - and charges them interest. A café owner who wants £30,000 for a second shop does not have to find hundreds of savers willing to lend. The bank has already collected their deposits.

Without banks, this would be much harder, because:

  • savers want to be able to take their money out at short notice, while firms want to borrow for years
  • a single saver lending to a single firm could lose everything if that firm failed, whereas a bank spreads its lending across thousands of borrowers
  • most savers have no way of judging whether a business is likely to repay.

Across the economy, this means savings end up paying for investment. Investment raises firms' capacity and productivity, so the economy can produce more. Money left sitting idle does neither.

Role of Banks in Providing Credit

Credit: money lent to a household or firm that has to be paid back later, usually with interest.

Firms borrow from banks for three main reasons. A new business needs money to start up before it has any sales. An existing business may need to cover a gap between paying its bills and being paid by its customers. And a growing business may want to invest in more capacity than its profits can pay for.

Before lending, a bank checks how likely the borrower is to repay. It will usually ask for a business plan and a cash-flow forecast, look at the firm's past accounts and credit history, and ask what the money is for. The main types of credit - loans, overdrafts and trade credit - are covered in 1.4.3.

Banks make most of their profit from the gap between the interest they charge borrowers and the interest they pay savers. So a bank wants to lend, but only to borrowers it expects to pay it back.

Interest Rates

Interest rate: the cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it, shown as a percentage of the amount borrowed or saved per year.

A firm that borrows £30,000 at 8% a year pays £2,400 in interest in the first year, on top of repaying the loan itself. The higher the rate, the more each project has to earn to be worth borrowing for.

The general level of interest rates also links back to saving and investment. When rates are low, saving earns little and borrowing is cheap, so households tend to save less and firms invest more. When rates are high, the opposite happens.

Different borrowers pay different rates. The rate a bank charges depends on:

Factor Effect on the rate charged
Bank Rate Set by the Bank of England. When it rises, banks pay more to borrow themselves, so they charge more on new loans and overdrafts.
Risk of the borrower A new business with no track record is more likely to default than an established one, so the bank charges a higher rate to cover that chance.
Collateral A secured loan is cheaper than an unsecured one, because the bank can recover its money if the borrower fails to pay.
Length and type of loan Money lent for longer is exposed to more things going wrong. Overdrafts usually cost more than loans.

Bank Rate is not the rate a firm pays. It is the rate the Bank of England sets, and lenders add to it. When a case study says a small firm was charged far more than Bank Rate, explain why using risk and collateral, not just the cost of money.

Collateral

Collateral: an asset a borrower promises to a lender, which the lender can take and sell if the loan is not repaid.

Property is the most common collateral, but machinery, vehicles and stock can also be used. A loan backed by collateral is a secured loan. One without is an unsecured loan.

Collateral lowers the bank's risk, so it makes lending more likely and cheaper. That creates a problem for new and small firms. They often own few assets, so they have little to offer as security. The bank may then ask the owner for a personal guarantee, a promise to repay from their own money, sometimes backed by their home. If the business fails, the owner can lose personal assets as well as the business (see 1.4.2).
