Stakeholders and Their Objectives

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.1.3 - Stakeholders (Economic Agents) and Their Objectives. Students need to identify a business's stakeholders, explain what each group wants, analyse where those objectives clash, and weigh up the case for and against corporate social responsibility.

Who Stakeholders Are

Stakeholder: any person or group with an interest in a business, because its decisions affect them or their decisions affect it.

The spec also calls stakeholders economic agents: households, firms and the government, each making decisions about scarce resources. A single person can be several stakeholders at once. A supermarket worker who shops in the store and owns a few of its shares is an employee, a customer and an owner. Shareholders are only one kind of stakeholder, so keep the two words apart in answers.

Stakeholders are usually split into two groups:

  • Internal stakeholders are inside the business: owners, managers and employees.
  • External stakeholders are outside it but still affected: customers, suppliers, lenders, the local community and the government.

Stakeholder Objectives

Each group wants something different from the business. The table sets out the usual objectives.

Stakeholder Type What they want
Owners and shareholders Internal Profit, dividends and a rising share price. Sole traders also want a decent income and some independence.
Managers Internal Higher salaries and bonuses, promotion, status and job security, often linked to how big the business grows.
Employees Internal Good pay, safe working conditions, training, and confidence that their job will still be there next year.
Customers External Low prices, good quality, reliable service and a choice of products.
Suppliers External Regular orders, fair prices and being paid on time.
Lenders External Interest paid and loans repaid in full, so they want the business to stay solvent.
Local community External Local jobs and spending, with as little noise, traffic and pollution as possible.
Government External Tax revenue, employment and growth, and firms that obey the law on wages, safety, competition and the environment.

Stakeholder Conflicts

A business cannot give every group everything it wants, because its resources are scarce. More for one stakeholder often means less for another. Common clashes:

  • Shareholders and employees over pay. A pay rise raises costs, which cuts the profit available for dividends. Replacing staff with automated checkouts pleases shareholders but threatens jobs.
  • The firm and the local community over expansion. A new distribution warehouse brings jobs, but also lorries on village roads and lost green space. Residents may oppose the planning application even if some of them would work there.
  • Customers and shareholders over price. Customers want low prices, while owners want the higher margins that come from raising them.
  • Shareholders and managers over objectives. Managers may chase growth or their own pay when owners want profit. This is the principal-agent problem from business objectives.

Interests do not always clash. A business that is doing well can pay higher wages, more tax and bigger dividends all at once, and in the long run satisfied customers and staff tend to make it more profitable. Where there is a conflict, the group with the most power usually wins. Shareholders can vote out directors, a trade union can threaten strike action, and the government can pass laws.

Corporate Social Responsibility

Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a business taking account of the effects of its decisions on society and the environment, and not just on its owners, beyond what the law requires.

Examples include paying suppliers in developing countries a fair price, cutting packaging and carbon emissions, and funding local projects.

Arguments for CSR Arguments against CSR
A good reputation attracts customers, some of whom will pay more for ethical products. It raises costs, which means lower profit or higher prices.
It helps recruit and keep staff who care about how their employer behaves. A business's job is to make a profit within the law. Spending shareholders' money on causes they did not choose is unfair to them.
Acting before the law forces it can head off tougher regulation later. Some firms exaggerate their good deeds for publicity, known as greenwashing when the claims are environmental.

Whether CSR pays off depends on the business. It is easier to justify for a well-known brand whose customers notice how it behaves than for a small firm competing only on price.
