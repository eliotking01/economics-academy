The Wider Economic Environment

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.2.5 - The Wider Economic Environment. Students need to explain what changes in interest rates, exchange rates, taxation, unemployment and inflation mean for firms: their costs, the demand for their products and their plans to invest.

Why the Wider Economy Matters to a Business

A business controls its own prices, products and staff. It does not control the economy around it. The Bank of England sets Bank Rate, which other interest rates follow. The government sets taxes, and currency markets decide exchange rates.

A change in any of these can hit a firm from two sides at once: what it costs to produce, and how much customers want to buy. That in turn affects whether it is willing to invest.

How Each Change Affects a Firm

Change Costs Demand and revenue Investment
Interest rates rise Repayments on loans and overdrafts go up Households with mortgages and credit cards spend less; goods bought on credit, such as cars, are hit hardest A project paid for with borrowed money must earn more to be worth it, so some are delayed
The pound strengthens Imported materials and components get cheaper Exports cost more abroad, so overseas sales fall; cheaper imports compete harder at home Exporters facing lower sales hold back on expanding
Taxes rise Employer National Insurance, VAT and excise duties add to costs or squeeze margins Higher income tax leaves households less to spend Higher corporation tax leaves less profit to reinvest
Unemployment rises Easier to recruit, and less pressure to raise wages Spending falls, especially on luxuries; discount retailers and own brands can gain Weak demand makes extra capacity hard to justify
Inflation rises Materials, energy, rent and wages all cost more If prices rise faster than wages, real incomes fall and households buy less Moving costs and prices make returns hard to predict, so some firms wait

A move the other way, such as a cut in interest rates or a weaker pound, broadly reverses each effect.

Interest Rates

Interest rate: the cost of borrowing money, and the reward for saving it, shown as a percentage per year.

On a £200,000 business loan at a variable rate, a rise of one percentage point adds £2,000 a year in interest. Not every firm loses, though. A business with cash in the bank earns more on it, and one that invests out of retained profit has no loan to worry about.

Exchange Rates

Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another, such as £1 = $1.30.

The memory aid is SPICED: a Strong Pound makes Imports Cheap and Exports Dear. A UK firm selling a £20 product to the US charges an American buyer $25 at £1 = $1.25. If the pound strengthens to £1 = $1.40, the same product costs $28.

When the pound weakens, an exporter can cut its foreign price to win sales, or keep it and take a bigger margin. Many firms both import inputs and export output, so the net effect depends on which matters more to them.

Taxation

Employer National Insurance is a recent example of a tax on employing people. From 6 April 2025 the employer rate rose from 13.8% to 15%, and the earnings level at which employers start paying fell from £9,100 to £5,000 a year. The lower threshold hits low-paid and part-time staff hardest relative to their pay, so employers with many of them, such as shops and restaurants, bear more of the rise.

Unemployment and Inflation

Unemployment: people who are able and willing to work, and looking for a job, but without one (unit 2.5.4).

Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured in the UK by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) (unit 2.5.3).

Rising inflation often brings higher interest rates too, as the Bank of England tries to bring it down. Firms with strong brands can usually pass higher costs on to customers. Firms in price-competitive markets often cannot, so their margins shrink, and exporters lose sales if UK prices rise faster than their rivals' abroad.

The effect always depends on the business. A rise in interest rates barely touches a debt-free firm selling basic groceries, but can sink a heavily borrowed car dealer. Before you answer, check the extract for the firm's debts, what it imports and exports, and whether it sells necessities or luxuries. That is where your evaluation comes from.
