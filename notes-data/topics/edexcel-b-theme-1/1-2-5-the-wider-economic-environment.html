The Wider Economic Environment

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.2.5 - The Wider Economic Environment. Students need to explain what changes in interest rates, exchange rates, taxation, unemployment and inflation mean for firms: their costs, the demand for their products and their plans to invest.

Why the Wider Economy Matters to a Business

A business controls its own prices, products and staff. It does not control the economy around it. The Bank of England sets Bank Rate, which other interest rates follow. The government sets taxes, and currency markets decide exchange rates. Unemployment and inflation depend on the whole economy.

When any of these changes, it can hit a firm from two sides at once: what it costs to produce, and how much customers want to buy. For each change below, ask what happens to the firm's costs, its demand and revenue, and its willingness to invest.

Interest Rates

Interest rate: the cost of borrowing money, and the reward for saving it, shown as a percentage per year.

A rise in interest rates means:

  • Higher costs for firms with loans or overdrafts on variable rates. On a £200,000 business loan, a rise of one percentage point adds £2,000 a year in interest.
  • Lower demand, as households with mortgages and credit cards have less to spend, and saving becomes more attractive than spending. Products often bought on credit, such as cars, furniture and new kitchens, are hit hardest.
  • Less investment, because a new machine or building paid for with borrowed money now has to earn more just to cover the interest.

Not every firm loses. A business with cash in the bank earns more on it, and one that pays for investment out of retained profit has no loan to worry about. A cut in interest rates works the other way on all three effects.

Exchange Rates

Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another, such as £1 = $1.30.

The memory aid is SPICED: a Strong Pound makes Imports Cheap and Exports Dear. Take a UK firm selling a £20 product to the US. At £1 = $1.25 it costs an American buyer $25. If the pound strengthens to £1 = $1.40, the same product costs $28, and some buyers switch to cheaper rivals.

So a stronger pound:

  • Hurts exporters, who must either accept lower sales abroad or cut their sterling price and their profit margin.
  • Helps importers, since materials and components bought from abroad cost fewer pounds.
  • Hurts UK firms competing with imports at home, as foreign goods on UK shelves become cheaper.

A weaker pound reverses each effect. An exporter then has a choice: cut its foreign price to win sales, or keep the foreign price where it was and take a bigger margin on each unit. How much a lower price lifts sales depends on how price-sensitive foreign buyers are. Many firms both import inputs and export output, so the net effect depends on which matters more to them.

Taxation

Different taxes reach a business by different routes:

  • Taxes on profit (corporation tax): a higher rate leaves less profit to pay owners or to reinvest, which can reduce investment.
  • Taxes on employing people (employer National Insurance): a higher rate raises the cost of every worker. From 6 April 2025 the employer rate rose from 13.8% to 15%, and the earnings level at which employers start paying fell from £9,100 to £5,000 a year. The lower threshold hits low-paid and part-time staff hardest relative to their pay, so employers with many of them, such as shops and restaurants, bear more of the rise.
  • Taxes on spending (VAT and excise duties): a rise either pushes up the price customers pay, reducing demand, or is absorbed by the firm, cutting its margin.
  • Taxes on income (income tax): a rise leaves households with less disposable income, so demand falls.

Unemployment

Unemployment: people who are able and willing to work, and looking for a job, but without one. It is covered in full in unit 2.5.4.

When unemployment rises:

  • Demand falls for most products, as people who lose their jobs cut back and those still in work save more in case they are next. Luxuries and eating out suffer most. Discount retailers and cheaper own-brand goods can gain as shoppers trade down.
  • Recruiting gets easier and cheaper. More people apply for each vacancy and there is less pressure to raise wages to hold on to staff.

Low unemployment reverses both: strong demand, but a struggle to fill jobs and pressure to pay more.

Inflation

Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured in the UK by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI). It is covered in full in unit 2.5.3.

When inflation rises:

  • Costs rise. Raw materials, energy and rent go up, and staff ask for pay rises to keep up with the cost of living.
  • Demand can fall. If prices rise faster than wages, households' real incomes shrink and they buy less. The Bank of England may also raise interest rates to bring inflation down, which brings the interest rate effects above into play as well.
  • Planning gets harder. When costs and prices keep moving, it is difficult to set prices, agree contracts or judge whether an investment will pay off, so some firms delay investing.
  • Exporters lose competitiveness if UK prices rise faster than prices in the countries they sell to.

Firms with strong brands can often pass higher costs on to customers. Firms in price-competitive markets usually cannot, so inflation squeezes their margins.

The effect always depends on the business. A rise in interest rates barely touches a debt-free firm selling basic groceries, but can sink a heavily borrowed car dealer. Before you answer, check the extract for the firm's debts, what it imports and exports, and whether it sells necessities or luxuries. That is where your evaluation comes from.
