Theme 4 4.1.2 Barriers to Entry

Bias

Anything that makes research results lean one way, so they no longer give a true picture of the market. It can creep in at several points:

  • Who is asked. Surveying only a firm's social media followers misses everyone who has never heard of it.
  • How the question is worded. "Wouldn't you love a gym on your doorstep?" pushes people towards yes.
  • What people say. Respondents often give the answer they think is wanted, and saying you would buy something is not the same as buying it.
  • Who reads the results. An owner keen on the idea may pay most attention to the answers that support it.

Theme 2 2.5.2 Circular Flow of Income, Expenditure and Output

Money lent to a household or firm that has to be paid back later, usually with interest.

The shared beliefs, customs and way of life of a group of people.

The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods and services, plus income such as profits and interest earned abroad and transfers such as foreign aid.

Theme 2 2.6.1 Possible Macroeconomic Objectives

Also covered in 3.1.5

D

Deflation

A sustained fall in the general price level, so the rate of inflation is negative.

Theme 2 2.5.3 Inflation

Demand

The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a given period of time.

Theme 1 1.3.1 Demand

Unemployment caused by too little aggregate demand. It rises in a recession, so it is also called cyclical unemployment (unit 2.5.1).

Theme 2 2.5.4 Employment and Unemployment

Inflation caused by aggregate demand rising faster than the economy can increase its output.

Theme 2 2.5.3 Inflation

Demand-side factor

Anything about the customers in a market, such as their culture, tastes or language, that affects how much of a product they want to buy.

Theme 3 3.3.2 Demand-Side Factors in Global Markets

Demand-side policies

Policies that change the level of aggregate demand (AD) to steer growth, unemployment and inflation.

Theme 4 4.4.2 Demand-Side Policies

A good or service with negative externalities in its consumption or production, so it is over-provided by the free market. Cigarettes, alcohol and gambling are examples.

Theme 4 4.3.1 Market Failure in Society

Depreciation

A fall in the value of a currency against another, such as the pound falling from $1.30 to $1.20.

Theme 2 2.4.4 Exchange Rates

Also covered in 3.1.5

Demand for something because of the demand for what it helps to produce. Firms want workers for the goods and services they make, not for their own sake.

Theme 3 3.5.2 Wage Rates

Developed economy

A high-income economy, also called a mature economy, where most people work in services and growth is slow but steady.

Theme 2 2.4.2 Developed, Emerging and Developing Economies

Developing economy

A low-income economy where many people work in farming and literacy and life expectancy are lower.

Theme 2 2.4.2 Developed, Emerging and Developing Economies

Digital economy

Economic activity that runs on the internet and digital technology, such as online shopping, apps, streaming and social media.

Theme 2 2.1.4 How the Digital Economy Affects Markets and Firms

Diseconomies of scale

The rise in long-run average cost when a firm grows beyond its minimum efficient scale.

Theme 2 2.1.1 Growth

Theme 2 2.5.3 Inflation

Household income after income tax and National Insurance, plus any state benefits. It is what people have to spend.

Theme 3 3.2.2 Assessing the Potential of Different Economies

How a product gets from the producer to the customer, and where customers can buy it.

Theme 2 2.2.3 Types of Non-Price Competition

Splitting production into separate tasks and giving each worker one task, or a small set of tasks, to do over and over.

Theme 1 1.2.4 Specialisation

A market that changes quickly, with new products, new technology and shifting tastes, such as smartphones, fashion or video games.

Theme 1 1.3.6 The Competition

E

How simple, quick and cheap it is to set up and run a firm in a country, from registering the company and getting permits to paying tax and enforcing contracts.

Theme 3 3.2.2 Assessing the Potential of Different Economies

Economic agents

The individuals, firms and governments whose decisions make up the economy.

Theme 3 3.6.3 The Impact of Inequality on Economic Agents

Economic cycle

The repeated rise and fall in economic activity, measured by real GDP, around the economy's long-term trend. It is also called the trade cycle or business cycle.

Theme 2 2.5.1 The Economic Cycle

Economic development

An improvement in people's living standards and welfare, measured by health, education and freedom from poverty as well as income.

Theme 3 3.6.2 Reducing Poverty

Economic growth

An increase in real GDP, the value of an economy's output adjusted for inflation.

Theme 2 2.4.2 Developed, Emerging and Developing Economies

Also covered in 2.3.1

Economic shock

An unexpected event that suddenly changes demand or costs across the economy.

Theme 4 4.5.1 Risks and Uncertainty

Economies of Scale

The fall in long-run average cost that comes from producing on a larger scale.

Theme 2 2.1.1 Growth

Also covered in 4.1.2

The value of a currency against a basket of other currencies, with each weighted by how much the country trades with it. It is also called the trade-weighted exchange rate.

Theme 2 2.4.4 Exchange Rates

Emerging economy

An economy moving from developing to developed, with fast growth, expanding industry and rising incomes.

Theme 2 2.4.2 Developed, Emerging and Developing Economies

Employment

People doing paid work, whether full-time, part-time or self-employed. In UK figures, one hour of paid work a week is enough to count.

Theme 2 2.5.4 Employment and Unemployment

Enterprise

The willingness and ability to organise the other factors of production and to take the risk of running a business.

Theme 1 1.2.3 Factors of Production

Entrepreneur

A person who spots a business opportunity, brings together the resources needed to exploit it and accepts the risk that it might fail.

Theme 1 1.2.1 Role of an Entrepreneur in the Economy

Environmental externality

An external cost or benefit that affects the natural environment, such as air, water, land or the climate.

Theme 4 4.3.2 Externalities

Equilibrium Price

The price at which the quantity consumers want to buy equals the quantity producers want to sell. It is also called the market-clearing price, because nothing is left unsold and no buyer who will pay that price goes without.

Theme 1 1.3.3 Price Determination

Equities

Shares in a company, each giving its owner part-ownership and a share of any profits paid out as dividends.

Theme 4 4.5.2 The Role of the Financial Sector

Ethnocentric approach

The domestic approach. The firm sells the same product abroad, in the same way, as it does at home.

Theme 3 3.3.1 Responding to Global Demand

Eurozone

The group of EU countries that use the euro as their currency, with interest rates set for all of them by the European Central Bank (ECB).

Theme 3 3.1.5 Exchange Rate Changes

Excess Demand

When the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied, because the price is below equilibrium. It shows up as a shortage.

Theme 1 1.3.3 Price Determination

Excess Supply

When the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded, because the price is above equilibrium. It shows up as a surplus.

Theme 1 1.3.3 Price Determination

Exchange Rate

The price of one currency in terms of another, such as £1 = $1.30.

Theme 1 1.2.5 The Wider Economic Environment

Also covered in 2.4.4, 3.2.2

Exchange rate risk

The risk that a change in the exchange rate alters the value of a payment a firm has agreed to make or receive in a foreign currency.

Theme 4 4.5.1 Risks and Uncertainty

Exports

Goods and services produced in one country and sold to buyers in another, so money flows into the exporting country.

Theme 2 2.4.3 International Trade

Extension strategy

Action taken to lengthen a product's life by lifting sales when they level off or start to fall.

Theme 2 2.1.3 Research and Development (R&D) and Innovation

External benefits

Gains that go to third parties, who do not pay for them.

Theme 1 1.5.1 Market Failure and Externalities

External costs

Costs that fall on a third party, someone outside the transaction, who is not compensated for them. Noise, pollution and congestion are the usual examples.

Theme 1 1.5.1 Market Failure and Externalities

External economies of scale

Cost savings a firm gets from the growth of its whole industry, often in one area, not from its own size.
