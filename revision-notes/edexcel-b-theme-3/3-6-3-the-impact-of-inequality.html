The Impact of Inequality on Economic Agents

Edexcel B · Theme 3 · 3.6.3 Updated 20 October 2026

Written by Eliot King — First-Class BSc (Hons) Economics, University of Bath · 6+ years teaching A-Level Economics · Edexcel A, Edexcel B, AQA and OCR

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 3.6.3 - The Impact of Inequality on Economic Agents. Students need to analyse how inequality affects individuals, firms (including the link between low income and low productivity) and the economy as a whole.

On this page

  1. Inequality and Economic Agents
  2. The Impact on Individuals
  3. The Impact on Firms: Low Income and Low Productivity
  4. The Impact on the Economy

Inequality and Economic Agents

Economic agents: the individuals, firms and governments whose decisions make up the economy.

Some inequality is expected: people who train for years or take risks earn more, and that reward gives others a reason to do the same. The questions are how wide the gap is and whether it comes from effort or from the family someone was born into.

Unit 3.6.1 shows how inequality is measured.

The Impact on Individuals

For people on low incomes:

  • Health: low income → cold or damp housing and a poorer diet → more illness and shorter lives → more time off work and lower earnings.
  • Education and opportunity: parents can't pay for books, tutoring or a move near a good school → children leave school with fewer qualifications → low-paid jobs as adults, so low income passes from one generation to the next.
  • Debt: no savings to fall back on → a broken boiler means borrowing at high interest → less left each month.

For people on high incomes:

  • Wealth builds: high earners can save → savings, shares and property earn interest, dividends and rent → their income and wealth grow further.
  • Incentives: the chance of higher pay → people train, work longer hours or start businesses → higher incomes for them and more output for the economy.

The effect on individuals depends on:

  • Public services: free healthcare and schools, such as the NHS, protect poorer families from the worst effects.
  • Social mobility: if a child from a poor family can still reach a well-paid job, inequality matters less than if incomes are fixed by background.

The Impact on Firms: Low Income and Low Productivity

Low income and low productivity feed each other. Productivity is output per worker (unit 2.3.1):

  • Low pay lowers productivity: low income → poorer health, stress and long journeys to work → more absence and less effort → less output per worker and higher unit costs for the firm.
  • Low productivity keeps pay low: each worker produces little → the firm can't afford to pay more → wages stay low → workers can't afford training to move up.
  • Staff turnover: low-paid staff leave for slightly better offers → the firm keeps recruiting and training → higher costs and less experienced staff.

A firm can break the cycle by paying more or training its staff. Better-paid workers are often more motivated and stay longer, so output per worker rises and some of the extra pay is recovered.

Inequality also changes what firms can sell:

  • Demand splits: incomes at the top rise faster than the rest → demand grows for luxury goods and for discount stores → firms in the middle of the market lose sales.

The effect on a firm depends on:

  • The type of work: where machines can replace low-paid staff, a firm may automate before it raises pay.
  • Its market: a luxury brand gains from a growing number of high earners; a mid-market chain loses.

The Impact on the Economy

  • Lower spending: low earners spend almost all of any extra income, high earners save more of it → the more income goes to the top, the less is spent → lower aggregate demand and slower growth.
  • Wasted talent: able children from poor families don't get the education to match their ability → a less skilled workforce → lower productivity and slower long-run growth.
  • Government finances: more people on low incomes → more spent on benefits and healthcare, and less collected in income tax → less to spend on education or infrastructure, or higher taxes elsewhere.
  • Social costs: large gaps → more crime and social unrest → political instability → firms invest less (unit 3.2.2).
  • Incentives and saving: the reward for success → more enterprise, and high earners' savings fund investment through banks → faster growth.

The overall effect depends on:

  • How large the gap is: some inequality keeps incentives strong; very wide gaps bring more of the costs.
  • Its cause: inequality from skill and effort can raise output; inequality from background wastes talent.
  • The government's response: taxes, benefits and public services narrow the gap (unit 3.6.4), but can weaken incentives if pushed too far.

Don't argue that all inequality is bad. A strong answer weighs the incentive effect against the costs to health, skills and demand, then judges which is bigger in the case given. The size of the gap and whether people can move up are usually what decide it.
