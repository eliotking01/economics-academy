Demand-Side Factors in Global Markets

Edexcel B · Theme 3 · 3.3.2 Updated 20 October 2026

Written by Eliot King — First-Class BSc (Hons) Economics, University of Bath · 6+ years teaching A-Level Economics · Edexcel A, Edexcel B, AQA and OCR

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 3.3.2 - Demand-Side Factors in Global Markets. Students need to explain how culture, tastes, language and translation affect demand for a firm's products abroad, and how firms adapt their marketing for niche and mass markets in response.

On this page

  1. What Demand-Side Factors Are
  2. Cultural and Social Factors
  3. Information and Communication Factors
  4. How Firms Respond to Demand-Side Factors

What Demand-Side Factors Are

Demand-side factor: anything about the customers in a market, such as their culture, tastes or language, that affects how much of a product they want to buy.

A product that sells well in the UK can fail abroad because customers there want something different, or read its marketing differently.

Cultural and Social Factors

Cultural Differences

Culture: the shared beliefs, customs and way of life of a group of people.

Culture shapes what people buy and how they expect it to be sold. Religion affects diet, which is why McDonald's sells no beef or pork in India (unit 3.3.1). It also affects what an advert can show, from alcohol to how people dress.

Different Tastes

Tastes differ even between similar countries: how sweet, salty or spicy food should be, portion sizes, favourite flavours. A UK crisp maker may find its salt and vinegar flavour sells poorly in a country that prefers spicy snacks.

When a firm ignores cultural and social factors:

  • The product misses: it does not suit local tastes or breaks a local custom → few customers buy it → low sales → the cost of entering the market is never recovered.
  • The brand suffers: an advert or pack offends local customers → complaints spread on social media → the brand's image is damaged → lower sales in that market, and possibly in others.

Information and Communication Factors

Language

Labels, packaging, websites, adverts and customer service all need to work in the local language. Some countries have more than one official language, so a single pack may need to carry two or three.

Unintended Meanings

A brand name chosen in English can sound like a rude or silly word in another language. A hand gesture, colour or image that is friendly in the UK can mean something quite different elsewhere.

Inappropriate or Inaccurate Translations

A slogan translated word for word can lose its meaning, because jokes and sayings rarely carry across. Instructions or ingredients translated wrongly are worse, as customers may use the product unsafely.

When a firm gets communication wrong:

  • Sales: a name or slogan is confusing or laughable → customers don't take the product seriously → lower sales, plus the cost of new packaging and adverts to relaunch it.
  • Legal and safety risks: a label is translated wrongly → it breaks local labelling law or misleads customers → fines, product recalls and a damaged brand.

How Firms Respond to Demand-Side Factors

Firms cut these risks with market research in each country (unit 1.3.5): testing names, flavours and adverts on local customers before launch, and using native speakers to translate. Many also hire local staff or agents who know the market.

How far a firm then adapts its marketing depends on whether it sells to a mass market or a niche (unit 2.2.1).

Mass market: a large market in which many firms sell similar products to customers with broadly the same needs.

Niche market: a small, clearly defined part of a larger market, with its own particular needs.

  • A firm in a mass market: it sells to millions of customers in many countries → it keeps one global brand but changes the recipe, pack size or adverts for each region → it fits local tastes while keeping most of its economies of scale → high sales at a low margin.
  • A firm in a niche market: a UK maker of climbing equipment sells to climbers worldwide → these buyers want much the same thing wherever they live → little changes beyond the language → a small niche at home becomes a large market overall, sold at a high margin.

Whether adapting pays depends on:

  • Cost against extra sales: research, translation and new packaging cost money. In a small market, selling the standard product may earn more profit.
  • How different the market is: a UK firm moving into Ireland finds English widely spoken and many of the same tastes, so it needs few changes. Moving into Japan needs far more.

Name the factor, then finish the chain. "Tastes are different in China" earns little on its own. Explain what the difference does: the product sells poorly, so the firm adapts it, and adapting raises its costs. Then judge whether the extra sales are likely to cover that cost.
