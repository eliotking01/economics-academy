Price Determination

Edexcel B · Theme 1 · 1.3.3 Updated 7 October 2026

Written by Eliot King — First-Class BSc (Hons) Economics, University of Bath · 6+ years teaching A-Level Economics · Edexcel A, Edexcel B, AQA and OCR

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.3.3 - Price Determination. Students need to explain how equilibrium price and quantity are set, use diagrams to show excess demand and excess supply, explain how market forces remove them, use the model to analyse real-world changes, and assess the limits of the model.

On this page

  1. Equilibrium Price and Quantity
  2. Excess Demand and Excess Supply
  3. How Market Forces Remove Excess Demand and Supply
  4. Using Supply and Demand in Real-World Situations
  5. Limitations of the Supply and Demand Model

Equilibrium Price and Quantity

Put the demand curve from 1.3.1 and the supply curve from 1.3.2 on the same diagram and they cross at one point.

Equilibrium price: the price at which the quantity consumers want to buy equals the quantity producers want to sell. It is also called the market-clearing price, because nothing is left unsold and no buyer who will pay that price goes without.

The equilibrium quantity is the amount bought and sold at that price. Nobody sets either figure. They come from the decisions of all the buyers and sellers in the market, and once the market reaches them there is no pressure for the price to change.

Excess Demand and Excess Supply

At any price other than the equilibrium, the two quantities do not match.

Excess demand: when the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied, because the price is below equilibrium. It shows up as a shortage.

Excess supply: when the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded, because the price is above equilibrium. It shows up as a surplus.

On both diagrams the excess is the horizontal distance between the two curves at P1. Label it, and mark QD and QS on the quantity axis.

How Market Forces Remove Excess Demand and Supply

Excess demand pushes the price up. Shops sell out and queues form, so buyers are prepared to pay more and sellers see they can charge more. As the price rises, demand contracts and supply extends. That carries on until the two are equal again at Pe (the arrows in Figure 2).

Excess supply pushes the price down. Sellers are left with unsold stock, so they cut prices to clear it. As the price falls, demand extends and supply contracts until the market clears at Pe (the arrows in Figure 3).

No one has to organise this. Each buyer and seller reacting to the shortage or the surplus is enough to move the price.

Using Supply and Demand in Real-World Situations

When something shifts demand or supply, the old price no longer clears the market. A shortage or surplus appears at the old price, and market forces move the price until a new equilibrium is reached.

Change At the old price New P and Q Example
Demand shifts right Excess demand, so the price rises P up, Q up A heatwave and electric fans
Demand shifts left Excess supply, so the price falls P down, Q down Winter coats after a mild winter
Supply shifts right Excess supply, so the price falls P down, Q up A bumper strawberry harvest
Supply shifts left Excess demand, so the price rises P up, Q down The poor cocoa harvests of 2023/24 (see 1.3.2)

In an exam answer, work through the same steps each time: name the factor, say which curve shifts and which way, explain the shortage or surplus at the old price, then state the new price and quantity.

Limitations of the Supply and Demand Model

The model is a simplification. It predicts the direction a price will move, but real markets often behave differently:

  • Ceteris paribus rarely holds. In the real world several factors change at once. If demand and supply both rise, quantity goes up, but the price could go either way depending on which shift is bigger.
  • Time lags. Supply often cannot respond quickly. A farmer cannot grow more wheat until the next harvest, and new houses take years to plan and build, so shortages can last.
  • Imperfect information. Buyers and sellers do not know where the equilibrium is. They find it by trial and error. People also buy out of habit or on impulse, which the model leaves out.
  • Non-price factors. Many firms compete on quality, brand and service, and change prices only now and then. Contracts, printed price lists and customer loyalty all slow price changes down.
  • Markets that do not clear. Some prices are held away from equilibrium on purpose. Tickets for big concerts are often priced below what fans would pay, so excess demand remains and tickets sell out within minutes.
  • No sense of scale. The diagram tells you the price will rise, but not by how much. That depends on how steep the curves are.

Use the limits to evaluate. After analysing a change with a diagram, the evaluation marks come from asking whether the prediction will really happen. Pick the limit that fits the case: a time lag for housing or crops, two curves shifting at once when several things change together.
