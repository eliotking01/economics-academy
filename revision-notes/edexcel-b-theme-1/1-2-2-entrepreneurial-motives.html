Entrepreneurial Motives

Edexcel B · Theme 1 · 1.2.2 Updated 27 September 2026

Written by Eliot King — First-Class BSc (Hons) Economics, University of Bath · 6+ years teaching A-Level Economics · Edexcel A, Edexcel B, AQA and OCR

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.2.2 - Entrepreneurial Motives. Students need to explain why profit acts as an incentive to start a business, and the non-financial motives the spec names: an ethical stance, social entrepreneurship, independence and home working.

On this page

  1. Why People Start Businesses
  2. Profit as an Incentive
  3. Non-Financial Motives

Why People Start Businesses

Starting a business means giving up a steady wage, putting savings at risk and working long hours with no guarantee of success. The reasons people do it anyway are their motives.

Economists usually assume the motive is money, and for many founders it is. But plenty of people start businesses for other reasons, and most have a mix. The motive matters because it shapes how the business is run: what it charges, who it buys from and how fast it tries to grow.

Profit as an Incentive

Profit: the amount by which a business's total revenue exceeds its total costs.

Profit is the reward for enterprise. An entrepreneur takes a risk that employees don't, and the chance of a profit is what makes the risk worth taking. It works as an incentive in three ways:

  • It rewards the owner. Profit is the entrepreneur's income, and a successful business can earn far more than a salary would.
  • It pays for growth. Profit kept in the business can fund new equipment, extra staff or a second site without borrowing.
  • It sends a signal. High profits in a market tell other entrepreneurs there is money to be made, so new firms enter and resources move to where consumers want them. This is the price mechanism at work (unit 1.3.4).

Profit also has to beat the opportunity cost. Someone who leaves a £35,000-a-year job to run a business that makes £20,000 profit is £15,000 a year worse off in money terms. If they stay in business, something other than money is keeping them there.

Non-Financial Motives

These are reasons for starting a business that come from the owner's values or the way they want to live. The spec names four.

Ethical Stance

An entrepreneur with an ethical stance runs the business by moral principles, even when that costs more. Think of a clothing brand that uses only organic cotton and pays its suppliers above the going rate, or a cosmetics firm that refuses to test on animals. Costs are often higher, but some customers will pay a premium for a product they feel good about buying, so ethics can also be a selling point.

Social Entrepreneurship

Social enterprise: a business that trades to tackle a social or environmental problem and reinvests most of its profit in that aim.

A social entrepreneur still sells goods or services and still needs revenue above costs, but the surplus pays for the mission. The Big Issue is a well-known UK example. Founded in 1991, it gives people who are homeless or at risk of homelessness the chance to earn a legitimate income: vendors buy copies of the magazine and sell them on to the public at a higher price, keeping the difference.

Independence

Many people start a business to be their own boss. They choose what to work on, when to work and how the business is run, with no manager above them. The price of independence is that every decision, and every loss, is theirs. Some founders turn a hobby into a business for the same reason: control over work they enjoy.

Home Working

Running a business from home cuts costs sharply, as there is no shop or office to rent and no commute. It also fits around childcare or caring for a relative. Selling online has made this far easier: a candle maker, a tutor or a graphic designer can reach customers across the country from a spare room. The limits are space and the difficulty of taking on staff, so home-based businesses often stay small.

Don't treat profit and non-financial motives as either/or. A social enterprise that makes a loss cannot keep helping anyone, and an ethical brand may earn more because of its stance. When a question asks about an entrepreneur's motives, use the extract to judge which motive comes first for them, and explain how it still depends on the business covering its costs.
