The Wider Economic Environment

Edexcel B · Theme 1 · 1.2.5 Updated 15 October 2026

Written by Eliot King — First-Class BSc (Hons) Economics, University of Bath · 6+ years teaching A-Level Economics · Edexcel A, Edexcel B, AQA and OCR

Specification Coverage: Edexcel B unit 1.2.5 - The Wider Economic Environment. Students need to explain what changes in interest rates, exchange rates, taxation, unemployment and inflation mean for firms: their costs, the demand for their products and their plans to invest.

On this page

  1. Why the Wider Economy Matters to a Business
  2. How Each Change Affects a Firm
  3. Interest Rates
  4. Exchange Rates

Why the Wider Economy Matters to a Business

A business controls its own prices, products and staff. It does not control the economy around it. The Bank of England sets Bank Rate, which other interest rates follow. The government sets taxes, and currency markets decide exchange rates.

A change in any of these can hit a firm from two sides at once: what it costs to produce, and how much customers want to buy. That in turn affects whether it is willing to invest.

How Each Change Affects a Firm

Interest rates rise Repayments on loans and overdrafts go up Households with mortgages and credit cards spend less; goods bought on credit, such as cars, are hit hardest A project paid for with borrowed money must earn more to be worth it, so some are delayed
The pound strengthens Imported materials and components get cheaper Exports cost more abroad, so overseas sales fall; cheaper imports compete harder at home Exporters facing lower sales hold back on expanding

Interest Rates

Interest rate: the cost of borrowing money, and the reward for saving it, shown as a percentage per year.

A rise in interest rates reaches a firm in three ways:

  • Costs: rates rise → interest on variable-rate loans and overdrafts goes up → higher costs → lower profit for any firm that borrows. On a £200,000 business loan at a variable rate, a rise of one percentage point adds £2,000 a year in interest.
  • Demand: rates rise → mortgage and credit-card repayments go up, and saving pays more → households spend less → lower sales, worst for things often bought on credit, such as cars and furniture.
  • Investment: rates rise → borrowing to pay for a new machine or shop costs more → fewer projects earn enough to cover the interest → investment is delayed, and so are the jobs it would have created.

A cut in rates runs each chain the other way: cheaper borrowing, more spending and more projects worth funding.

How much a rise hurts depends on:

  • How much the firm borrows: a heavily borrowed firm is hit hardest. One that invests out of retained profit has no loan to worry about, and a business with cash in the bank earns more on it.
  • Fixed or variable rates: a fixed-rate loan protects a firm until the deal ends. Many households have fixed-rate mortgages too, so the fall in spending builds up over months, not weeks.

Exchange Rates

Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another, such as £1 = $1.30.

The memory aid is SPICED: a Strong Pound makes Imports Cheap and Exports Dear. A UK firm selling a £20 product to the US charges an American buyer $25 at £1 = $1.25. If the pound strengthens to £1 = $1.40, the same product costs $28.

When the pound strengthens:

  • Exporters lose: pound strengthens → UK goods cost more in foreign currency → overseas buyers switch to cheaper rivals → export sales and profit fall, putting jobs in exporting firms at risk.
  • Importers gain: pound strengthens → imported raw materials and components cost less in pounds → lower unit costs → higher profit margins, or room to cut prices.
  • Firms competing with imports lose: pound strengthens → foreign goods get cheaper in UK shops → UK producers lose sales at home.

When the pound weakens, each chain runs the other way. An exporter can cut its foreign price to win sales, or keep it and take a bigger margin. An importer's costs rise, and if it passes them on, its UK customers pay more.

The effect on a particular firm depends on:

  • Its mix of imports and exports: many firms both import inputs and export output, so the net effect depends on which matters more to them.
  • How price-sensitive its customers are: a strong brand can charge more abroad and keep most of its buyers. A firm selling a standard product cannot (price elasticity of demand, unit 2.2.1).
  • Time lags and duration: prices fixed in contracts take time to change, and buyers take time to switch supplier. A small move that reverses within weeks changes little; a lasting one can change where a firm buys and sells.

Taxation

A rise in tax reaches firms through four routes:

  • Taxes on employing people: employer National Insurance rises → each worker costs more → higher labour costs → the firm raises prices, accepts lower profit, holds down pay or hires fewer staff.
  • Taxes on spending: VAT or excise duty rises → the price customers pay goes up → sales fall, or the firm absorbs the tax and its margin shrinks.
  • Taxes on income: income tax rises → households keep less of their pay → they spend less → lower sales, especially of luxuries.
  • Taxes on profit: corporation tax rises → less profit after tax → less retained profit to reinvest → lower investment.

A tax cut reverses each one.

Employer National Insurance is a recent example. From 6 April 2025 the employer rate rose from 13.8% to 15%, and the earnings level at which employers start paying fell from £9,100 to £5,000 a year. That hits firms with many low-paid or part-time staff hardest, such as shops and restaurants.

How much any tax rise hurts also depends on whether the firm can pass it on. If customers will pay more without buying much less, it protects its margin. In a price-competitive market it has to absorb more of the tax itself.

Unemployment and Inflation

Unemployment: people who are able and willing to work, and looking for a job, but without one (unit 2.5.4).

Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured in the UK by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) (unit 2.5.3).

When unemployment rises:

  • Demand falls: people lose their jobs and others worry about theirs → household incomes and confidence fall → lower sales, especially of luxuries. Discount retailers and own brands can gain as shoppers trade down.
  • Labour gets cheaper: more people apply for each vacancy → easier to recruit and less pressure to raise wages → lower labour costs.

When unemployment falls, the second chain reverses: firms compete for scarce staff → they raise wages to recruit and keep them → higher costs and lower margins.

When inflation rises:

  • Costs: materials, energy and wages cost more → higher unit costs → lower margins unless the firm raises its prices.
  • Demand: prices rise faster than wages → real incomes fall → households buy less → lower sales.
  • Interest rates: the Bank of England raises rates to bring inflation down → the interest-rate chains above follow.
  • Exports: UK prices rise faster than rivals' abroad → UK goods look expensive overseas → exporters lose sales.

How much inflation hurts depends on:

  • Pricing power: firms with strong brands can usually pass higher costs on to customers. Firms in price-competitive markets often cannot, so their margins shrink.
  • Whether wages keep up: if pay rises as fast as prices, real incomes hold up and spending falls less.

The effect always depends on the business. A rise in interest rates barely touches a debt-free firm selling basic groceries, but can sink a heavily borrowed car dealer. Before you answer, check the extract for the firm's debts, what it imports and exports, and whether it sells necessities or luxuries. That is where your evaluation comes from.
