Sample cards

Define purchasing power parity (PPP). How has economic power shifted towards China, India, Africa and other emerging markets since 2000?

Purchasing power parity (PPP) — a way of comparing countries' output that adjusts for differences in prices, so that a dollar buys the same amount in each country.

Economic power comes from the size of an economy: a bigger one buys and sells more and has more say in trade talks. Measured at PPP, between 2000 and 2025:

  • China: its share of world GDP more than doubled, and it has been the world's largest economy since 2014. Its growth came from manufacturing for export.
  • India: its share almost doubled, and it is now the third largest economy. Its growth has leaned more on services sold to firms abroad, such as IT.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: its share barely moved, but its population almost doubled, so it is a fast-growing market and workforce. Many of its economies rely on exporting oil and metals, so their growth rises and falls with world prices.
  • Other emerging markets: Vietnam and Indonesia have attracted factories as wages in China rise.

Over the same period, the shares of the US and the UK fell.

Define nominal value and real value. State the formula for converting a nominal value into a real one.

Real value=Nominal valuePrice index×100\text{Real value} = \frac{\text{Nominal value}}{\text{Price index}} \times 100Real value=Price indexNominal value​×100

Nominal value — a value measured in the prices of the time, with no adjustment for inflation.

Real value — a value adjusted for inflation, so it shows what has happened to the amount bought or produced.

Divide the nominal value by a price index and multiply by 100. The answer is in the prices of the price index's base year.

Comparing incomes over time means removing the effect of rising prices first. If wages triple while prices triple, real wages have not changed.

Define a tariff. Sketch its effect on a market open to world trade. Who gains, who loses, and where is the welfare loss?

Tariff — a tax on imported goods.

Without the tariff, the country imports as much as it wants at the world price, Sworld (P1): home producers supply Q1, consumers buy Q4 and imports fill the gap. The tariff raises the price of imports to Sworld+tariff (P2).

  • Home producers gain: the price they can charge rises → they expand output from Q1 to Q2 → a gain in producer surplus (the green area), and more jobs.
  • Consumers lose: they pay the higher price → they buy Q3 instead of Q4 → lower real incomes and less choice.
  • The government gains: it collects the tariff on each of the Q2–Q3 units still imported (the purple rectangle).

Imports shrink from Q1–Q4 to Q2–Q3. The two red triangles are a welfare loss. The left one is the cost of making goods at home that could be imported more cheaply. The right one is the lost benefit to consumers who no longer buy at all.
