Sample cards

Define economies of scale and diseconomies of scale.

Economies of scale — the fall in long-run average cost that comes from producing on a larger scale.

Diseconomies of scale — the rise in long-run average cost when a firm grows beyond its minimum efficient scale.

Both are about average cost, the cost of each unit. A growing firm spends more in total, but if its output rises faster than its costs, each unit gets cheaper to make.

Diseconomies come from coordination problems, as more sites, departments and products are harder to plan and control, and weaker motivation, as each worker feels like a small part of a big organisation.

State the formula for PED. Why is the answer negative, and how do you classify the value?

PED=% change in quantity demanded% change in price\text{PED} = \frac{\%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{\%\text{ change in price}}PED=% change in price% change in quantity demanded​

Work out each percentage change from the original figure, then divide. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so the answer is negative.

Ignore the minus sign when you classify. What matters is the size of the number:

  • 0: perfectly inelastic. Quantity demanded does not change at all.
  • Between 0 and 1: price inelastic. Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price.
  • 1: unitary elastic. Quantity changes by the same percentage as price.
  • Greater than 1: price elastic. Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price.
  • Infinite: perfectly elastic. Any rise in price and sales fall to zero.

The number also gives the size of the response: a PED of −0.5 means a 10% price rise cuts quantity demanded by 5%.

Sketch the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve. Label economies of scale, diseconomies of scale and the minimum efficient scale.

Costs go up the side and quantity along the bottom. The LRAC curve is U-shaped.

  • On the left, average cost falls as output rises: economies of scale.
  • The lowest point marks the minimum efficient scale (MES) — the lowest output at which a firm reaches the lowest possible long-run average cost.
  • Beyond the MES, average cost rises again: diseconomies of scale.

A firm already at its MES gains no more cost savings from growing, and may start to face diseconomies.
