99 cards · 19 topics · Edexcel B Theme 4: Making Markets Work

Sample cards

Define imperfect competition and monopolistic competition. Compare the four market structures on the spectrum of competition.

Imperfect competition — any market that is not perfectly competitive, so firms have some power over their price. Monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly are all forms of imperfect competition.

Monopolistic competition — a market with many firms and low barriers to entry, where each firm sells a slightly different product. Hairdressers, cafés and takeaways in a town are typical examples. Where the Edexcel B specification lists imperfect competition alongside monopoly and oligopoly, it means monopolistic competition.

  • Perfect competition: very many small firms, no barriers to entry, identical products → no power over price: firms are price takers.
  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, low barriers, slightly different products → a little power over price.
  • Oligopoly: a few large firms dominate, high barriers, identical or branded products → considerable power, but each firm watches its rivals.
  • Monopoly: one firm, very high barriers, no close substitutes → high power: a price maker.

The further a market sits towards monopoly, the more market power each firm has: the ability to raise its price without losing all its customers.

Define an oligopoly and a concentration ratio. State the formula for an n-firm concentration ratio, and give two limits of the measure.

Oligopoly — a market dominated by a few large, interdependent firms, protected by high barriers to entry.

Concentration ratio — the share of a market's total sales taken by its largest firms.

The higher the ratio, the more concentrated the market. A market is usually called an oligopoly when the top few firms hold most of it.

Limits:

  • It does not show how sales are shared among the top firms: a 4-firm ratio of 80% could be one giant and three small firms, or four equals.
  • It depends on how the market is drawn: a firm with a small share of national sales might dominate one town.

Define perfect competition. Sketch the market and the firm when demand has risen. What happens next, and where does the model fall short?

Perfect competition — a market with very many small firms selling homogeneous products, with perfect information and no barriers to entry or exit.

The market's demand and supply set the price, P1. Each firm is a price taker: a firm charging a penny more would sell nothing, so its demand curve is the horizontal line D=AR=MR=P1. It produces Q1, where MC cuts the price line.

  • Short run: P1 is above average cost C1 → the shaded rectangle is supernormal profit.
  • Entry: the profit signals an opportunity → new firms join, which is easy with no barriers → market supply rises → the price falls.
  • Long run: entry continues until the price equals the lowest average cost → each firm makes only normal profit.

Where it falls short: products are rarely identical, information is not perfect, entry is rarely free, and economies of scale mean firms are not all small. With every idea copied at once and profit competed away, firms also have little reason to innovate. It is a benchmark, not a description of real markets.
